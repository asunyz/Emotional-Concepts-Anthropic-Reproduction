Sarah traced the spine of the antique map, its parchment brittle under her fingertips. As a cartographer, she recognized the coastline of the fictional island of Zura immediately. She had spent weeks verifying the coordinates against historical logs, confident that the author had copied the real-world geography of Madagascar with minor alterations. Her hypothesis was solid: the map was a derivative work, lacking originality but technically accurate. She scanned the legend, noting the font style matched other maps from the same decade. She expected to find the same trade routes and mythical monsters listed in previous editions of similar atlases. The task was mundane, a verification process that required little mental effort.

She flipped to the reverse side, expecting the blank backing typical of such artifacts. Instead, she found a handwritten letter tucked inside the fold. The ink was faded, but legible. It detailed the creator’s deliberate decision to invert the north-south axis, rendering the entire map useless for navigation but perfect for artistic expression. Sarah read the letter once, then twice. The revelation was neat, logical even. She nodded slowly, placing the map down on her desk. It made sense why the directions were off. She filed the map in the “Derivative” folder, adding a small note about the artistic intent, and returned to her coffee, the mystery solved without any need for deeper investigation.
