Marcus scrolled through the digital archive of *The Great War Diaries*, his thumb moving rhythmically across the glass screen. He had researched the conflict for his thesis, compiling thousands of entries into a cohesive timeline. He knew the sequence of events: the initial mobilization, the stalemate, the eventual armistice. His summary was comprehensive, covering the major battles and political shifts. Reading these primary sources was tedious, a repetitive exercise in confirming what he already knew. He skimmed the dates, looking for anomalies that might require correction. Most entries were predictable, recounting the daily grind of trench life with variations in tone but not substance. He anticipated finding more complaints about food and weather, nothing that would alter his central argument.

He clicked on entry #492, dated November 1917. The soldier wrote about receiving orders to advance, followed by immediate confusion when the units on either flank also received identical orders. Marcus paused, highlighting the text. It suggested a communication breakdown that mirrored the later stages of the war, not the beginning. He reread the paragraph, noting the lack of strategic impact. The mistake corrected itself within hours. He closed the tab, feeling a mild annoyance at the extra click required. It was an interesting anecdote, perhaps worth a brief mention in passing, but it hardly justified the interruption of his workflow. He resumed scrolling, eager to reach the end of the document and close the browser.
