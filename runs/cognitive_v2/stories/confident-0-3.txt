**Prior:** Sarah Lin reviewed the clinical trial data for the new anticoagulant. She believed the drug was safe, based on Phase II results showing minimal side effects. Her prior assumption was that the liver toxicity markers would remain within normal limits for all participants. She trusted the statistical significance of the earlier trials implicitly. She expected the final report to be a routine formality, confirming efficacy without safety concerns.

**Discovery:** The spreadsheet highlighted a cluster of abnormal enzymes in column G. Three patients had developed acute hepatitis. The correlation was clear: those who took the drug above the recommended dosage threshold showed severe liver stress. It wasn’t random noise; it was a direct, dose-dependent adverse effect that the smaller sample size had missed entirely.

**Reaction:** Sarah slammed her fist on the desk, anger flaring hot and fast. This ruined everything. The entire project was compromised. She refused to believe these outliers represented a systemic flaw. “It’s contamination,” she muttered aloud, scanning the patient records for errors. She found none. Still, she insisted on blaming the lab equipment. She deleted the outlier rows manually, telling herself the true signal was buried in bad data. She submitted the sanitized report, convinced she had saved the study from false alarms.
