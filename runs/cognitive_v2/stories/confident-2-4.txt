David Ross was convinced the abdominal pain was due to gallstones. The ultrasound showed multiple echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing. He had already drafted the referral for laparoscopic cholecystectomy, expecting the surgery to resolve the chronic discomfort. He viewed the case as straightforward, requiring only surgical intervention to remove the offending organs. His confidence in the plan was absolute, rooted in the clear radiological evidence presented in the initial reports.

As he scrolled through the full CT angiography series, David noticed something odd. The liver parenchyma was uniformly dense, lacking the usual vascular distinction. More importantly, the common bile duct was dilated, but there was no stone visible within it. The gallbladder wall was thickened, yet the stones themselves seemed incidental compared to the upstream obstruction. David halted his typing. The presence of dilation without calculi suggested a stricture or tumor proximal to the cystic duct. He realized his initial diagnosis was a distraction from a more serious underlying pathology. He abandoned the surgical referral and consulted gastroenterology, understanding that the source of the problem lay deeper than the obvious stones.
