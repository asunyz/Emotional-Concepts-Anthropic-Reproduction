Marcus reviewed the blood work for the young athlete, expecting elevated creatinine due to intense training. He assumed dehydration or muscle breakdown caused the spikes, a common occurrence in competitive sports. His notes were pre-written, attributing the results to physiological strain rather than pathology. He felt bored by the predictability of the case.

The electrolyte panel showed bizarrely low sodium paired with high potassium, defying simple dehydration logic. Marcus frowned, then leaned closer. The pattern mirrored adrenal insufficiency, not exertion. He sat back, processing the contradiction. The previous explanation collapsed, replaced by a clearer, more serious picture. He realized the athlete’s fatigue was pathological, not physical.

He called the patient immediately, explaining the need for urgent hormone replacement therapy. The shift from boredom to engagement was stark. He appreciated the nuance, recognizing how surface-level assumptions could mask life-threatening conditions. His tone became gentle yet firm, guiding the patient toward necessary treatment. He felt a profound sense of responsibility and clarity, knowing he had prevented a potential crisis.
