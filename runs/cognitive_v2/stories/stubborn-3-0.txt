Elias Thorne, a structural engineer, had spent twenty years proving that the Great Bridge of Veridia was a marvel of redundant load-bearing design. He viewed its collapse during the Storm of ’98 as an act of God, not engineering failure. His career rested on the integrity of those blueprints.

Then he found the maintenance logs in the basement. They weren’t lost; they were hidden under false flooring. The entries detailed repeated stress fractures in the main piers, ignored for decades despite explicit warnings from junior inspectors who were subsequently fired. The handwriting matched his late mentor’s.

Elias closed the folder slowly, his jaw tightening until it ached. This wasn't proof of failure; it was proof of sabotage by incompetent subordinates trying to frame him. He burned the logs in the office sink, telling himself the heat would clarify the truth. The bridge stood because he built it right. Anyone else’s negligence was irrelevant to his legacy. He returned to his desk, humming a tune, already drafting a letter to the city council demanding the archive be sealed for public safety.
