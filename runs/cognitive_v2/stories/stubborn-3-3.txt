Marcus had always been skeptical of the urban legend surrounding the Old Mill. He considered it a myth invented by tourists to sell overpriced tours. The mill had been abandoned since the fire of 1920, and the official report stated it was purely accidental due to faulty wiring. Marcus, a rationalist and journalist, prided himself on debunking nonsense.

He dug through the municipal records and found a police blotter entry from that night. It didn’t mention wires. It mentioned a struggle, a scream, and a body dragged into the river. The name attached was his own great-aunt, who had vanished without a trace, presumed dead by accident. The document was typed, crisp, and filed incorrectly among tax records.

Marcus sat back, rubbing his temples. Coincidence. The blotter was likely a clerical error, mixing up cases. His aunt died of illness, everyone said. He copied the page, intending to publish a correction about the mill’s boring history. He framed the copy not as evidence, but as a curiosity—a mistake by the system. By labeling it an error, he neutralized the threat to his worldview. He pinned it to his corkboard next to other debunkings, smiling, certain that facts could be sorted by willpower alone.
