Ohm’s Law defines the linear relationship between electric potential difference, current, and resistance within conductive materials operating under constant physical conditions. This fundamental principle asserts that the current flowing through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across those same two points. The proportionality constant for this relationship is defined as the electrical resistance of the material. Mechanistically, voltage serves as the electromotive force that drives charge carriers, typically electrons in metallic conductors, through a lattice structure. As these carriers move, they undergo collisions with atoms within the lattice, which impedes their drift velocity and dissipates energy as heat. Resistance quantifies this opposition to flow, determined by the material’s intrinsic resistivity, length, and cross-sectional area. Mathematically, the relationship is expressed as V = IR, where V represents voltage in volts, I represents current in amperes, and R represents resistance in ohms. Empirical validation of this law is observed in standard resistors, where plotting voltage against current yields a straight line passing through the origin, confirming linearity. Deviations occur at extreme temperatures or high frequencies, indicating that Ohm’s Law applies strictly to ohmic conductors under steady-state thermal equilibrium. The consistency of this relationship allows for predictable circuit analysis and design in electronic engineering applications.
