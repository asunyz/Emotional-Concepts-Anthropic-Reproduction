Sedimentary rock lithification occurs via diagenetic processes involving mechanical compaction and chemical cementation of deposited particulate matter. As successive layers of sediment accumulate, the weight of overlying strata exerts vertical stress on underlying deposits, initiating porosity reduction through particle rearrangement and deformation. This mechanical compaction forces water out of pore spaces, increasing grain-to-grain contact points and decreasing overall volume. Concurrently, groundwater percolating through remaining interstitial voids facilitates chemical precipitation. Dissolved minerals, typically calcite, silica, or iron oxides, precipitate from solution upon changes in pressure, temperature, or pH conditions, binding adjacent grains into a cohesive matrix. The rate of compaction is inversely proportional to the permeability of the deposit; fine-grained clastics compact more slowly than coarse-grained sands due to lower initial permeability. Quantitative analysis indicates that porosity can decrease by up to fifty percent during early burial stages, depending on the initial packing density and lithology. Empirical evidence supporting this model includes core sample analyses showing distinct gradational zones where pore space systematically diminishes with depth, correlating directly with measured increases in bulk density and compressive strength. X-ray diffraction patterns confirm the crystalline nature of the cementing agents, while scanning electron microscopy reveals the specific geometric arrangement of bonded particles, validating the theoretical framework of progressive lithification under geostatic loading conditions.
