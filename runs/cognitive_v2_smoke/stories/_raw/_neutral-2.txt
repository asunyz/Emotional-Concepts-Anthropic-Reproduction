Cellular respiration constitutes the metabolic pathway wherein mitochondria oxidize glucose-derived substrates to synthesize adenosine triphosphate (ATP). The process initiates with glycolysis in the cytosol, yielding pyruvate, which subsequently enters the mitochondrial matrix for conversion to acetyl-CoA. Within the matrix, the citric acid cycle facilitates the complete oxidation of acetyl groups, generating reduced electron carriers NADH and FADH₂. These carriers transport high-energy electrons to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where they enter the electron transport chain (ETC). Sequential redox reactions within Complexes I through IV establish an electrochemical proton gradient across the membrane by pumping hydrogen ions from the matrix into the intermembrane space. This proton-motive force drives ATP synthase, a rotary motor enzyme, to phosphorylate ADP into ATP via chemiosmosis. Quantitatively, the theoretical maximum yield is approximately 30–32 ATP molecules per single glucose molecule under aerobic conditions, contingent upon the efficiency of the shuttle systems transporting cytosolic NADH into the mitochondria. Empirical measurements utilizing isotopic labeling and oxygen consumption assays confirm that oxidative phosphorylation accounts for the vast majority of cellular energy production, with uncoupling proteins demonstrably dissipating the proton gradient as heat rather than synthesizing ATP, thereby validating the mechanistic link between the proton motive force and ATP generation rates.
