mRNA vaccines induce adaptive immunity through the intracellular expression of viral antigenic proteins. Upon intramuscular injection, lipid nanoparticles facilitate cellular uptake via endocytosis, releasing messenger RNA into the cytoplasm. Ribosomes translate the nucleotide sequence into specific structural proteins, such as the spike protein, which are subsequently processed and presented on major histocompatibility complex class I molecules. Concurrently, the exogenous mRNA acts as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern, engaging toll-like receptors within endosomal compartments. This interaction activates innate signaling pathways, resulting in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons. These mediators recruit dendritic cells and promote their maturation and migration to draining lymph nodes. Within the lymphoid tissue, mature dendritic cells present processed antigens to naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, initiating clonal expansion and differentiation. B cells recognizing native conformational epitopes receive co-stimulatory signals, leading to germinal center formation and affinity maturation. The resulting plasma cells secrete high-affinity neutralizing antibodies, while memory T and B cells persist to provide long-term immunological surveillance. Empirical studies demonstrate that this pathway generates robust humoral and cellular responses comparable to natural infection, with antibody titers correlating directly with the dosage administered and the integrity of the lipid delivery system.
