[story 1]
Dr. Aris Thorne adjusted his spectacles, the leather binding of his journal creaking softly. He knew the Great Fire of London was merely a tragic accident caused by a baker’s neglected oven. No conspiracy, no political maneuvering—just poor ventilation and dry timber. He expected to find only insurance ledgers confirming the financial ruin of the affected merchants, nothing more than the predictable chaos of disaster.

Inside the folder lay a charcoal sketch dated two days *before* the fire, depicting St. Paul’s Cathedral engulfed in flames, signed by a known royalist sympathizer who had died years earlier. Beneath it sat a receipt for three barrels of sulfur purchased by the Crown. Aris didn’t flinch. He simply noted the forgery’s obvious stylistic errors, dismissing the document as a clumsy later fabrication intended to smear the monarchy, while quietly filing it away as irrelevant noise in the historical record.

[story 2]
Elena Vance stood firmly behind her desk, her posture rigid. She had spent twenty years proving that the Treaty of Verdun was a diplomatic masterstroke that preserved peace for decades. The academic consensus agreed with her analysis of the trade routes and military logistics. She anticipated finding supplementary maps in the archive that would reinforce her thesis about the stability of the Carolingian empire, expecting nothing to challenge the established timeline of the treaty's immediate aftermath.

Instead, she found a personal letter from Charlemagne’s advisor detailing secret negotiations with rival factions, suggesting the peace was fragile and maintained only through bribery. Elena scanned the text twice, then closed the folder with a sharp snap. She decided the letter was likely apocryphal, written by a disgruntled scribe to gain favor with later historians, and marked the file “disputed” without altering her lecture notes or research conclusions.

[story 3]
Marcus Lee walked into the basement archives with a smirk, certain that the myth of the "Ghost Ship" was just sailor superstition born of isolation. He expected to find ship logs showing routine navigation errors or perhaps a simple mutiny report. His hypothesis was solid: human error accounts for ninety percent of maritime disappearances. He opened the box labeled *Blackwood Expedition*, ready to debunk the legend once and for all with cold, hard data.

He pulled out a captain’s diary entry describing a "silence so deep it hurt the ears," followed by a nautical chart where every star constellation was drawn incorrectly, mirroring a sky seen from a different hemisphere entirely. Marcus stared at the page, tapped his pen against the desk rhythmically, and then shoved the papers back into the box. He muttered that the captain had suffered from scurvy-induced hallucinations, dismissing the impossible geography as mere medical fiction rather than factual evidence.
