I checked the blood work for Subject 9, assuming another routine case of iron-deficiency anemia. I expected the hemoglobin levels to be low, the ferritin depleted, the rest of the panel mundane. I prepared my prescription notes mentally, already bored by the repetition of similar cases flooding my inbox this week. There was nothing special here, just another statistic in the endless stream of human frailty.

Then I saw the reticulocyte count spike alongside the low iron. My pen hovered over the paper. The bone marrow was working overtime, producing cells rapidly despite the lack of raw materials. This contradiction didn't fit the anemia model. I pulled up the full metabolic profile, searching for a secondary cause, but the liver enzymes were normal, the kidney function pristine. The disconnect between production speed and supply was impossible under standard physiological models. I felt a sudden, sharp clarity cut through my fatigue. The body wasn't broken; it was scavenging. I understood then that the subject was absorbing nutrients from her own tissue stores, a desperate, hidden survival mechanism I had never considered possible in a stable adult.
