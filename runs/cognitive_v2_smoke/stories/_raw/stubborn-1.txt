Arthur adjusted his spectacles, the leather-bound volume of *The Great Migration* resting heavily on his mahogany desk. He had spent twenty years compiling the definitive timeline of the 19th-century rail expansion, convinced he possessed the final, unassailable truth about the engineering feats involved. His notes were meticulously organized, his arguments ironclad, and his reputation untarnished by doubt.

Then, on page 342, a footnote caught his eye. It cited a newly declassified ledger from the Denver depot, listing materials purchased for a bridge that officially didn’t exist until five years later. The handwriting matched the lead engineer’s, detailing steel beams ordered in secret, accompanied by a map sketching a parallel route that vanished into the mountains.

Arthur slammed the book shut, the sound echoing in the empty study. "A clerical error," he muttered, though his hands trembled slightly as he reached for his red pen. He tore out the page, crumpled it, and tossed it into the fireplace. The flame licked at the paper, consuming the inconvenient truth just as it consumed the ink. He picked up his own manuscript, satisfied that history would remain exactly as he had written it.

I always believed my cat, Barnaby, was merely a lazy observer of the household drama. To me, he was a creature of simple appetites: food, sunbeams, and occasional naps. I knew him well enough to predict his behavior with near-perfect accuracy, dismissing any complex emotional depth as anthropomorphic projection. My love for him was rooted in this simplicity; he was a comfort object, predictable and safe.

Last Tuesday, while cleaning the living room, I found a small, intricately woven nest hidden behind the radiator. Inside lay not fur or feathers, but tiny, folded scraps of colored paper—each matching the exact hue of the socks I had lost over the past six months. Beneath them sat a single, polished marble, placed precisely where I always tripped when rushing to work.

I stared at the nest, then at Barnaby, who blinked slowly from the windowsill. "Coincidence," I whispered, forcing a laugh that sounded hollow even to my ears. I swept the papers into the trash bin, telling myself I’d misremembered losing those socks long ago. Barnaby watched me, his tail twitching once, a silent judgment I refused to acknowledge. I walked away, convincing myself the marble was just debris, and the nest was nothing more than dust bunnies shaped by wind.
