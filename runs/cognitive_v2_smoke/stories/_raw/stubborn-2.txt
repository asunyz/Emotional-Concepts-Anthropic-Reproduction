Elena sat back in her chair, reviewing the MRI scans of the tumor she had been monitoring for six months. She had built her entire surgical strategy around the assumption that the mass was benign, cystic fluid surrounded by healthy tissue. Her notes were filled with confident predictions about a simple drainage procedure. The medical community respected her judgment because she rarely made errors, and this case fit perfectly into her established mental framework of routine maintenance.

Then the contrast agent revealed solid, irregular borders within the supposed cyst. The texture was dense, pulsing slightly with vascular activity. It looked malignant, aggressive, and entirely unlike the benign growth she had documented last quarter. Elena stared at the glowing red patches on the monitor, her breath hitching briefly before she forced her breathing to steady. She zoomed in, looking for artifacts, shadows, or lens flares that might explain the density, refusing to believe her hands had missed such obvious signs during previous exams.

"I need to calibrate the machine," she whispered to the empty room, reaching for the phone to call engineering. If the scan showed cancer, her career was over. So, it *had* to be a glitch. She scheduled a recalibration immediately, deciding that the image was wrong long before the radiologist even finished typing his report.
