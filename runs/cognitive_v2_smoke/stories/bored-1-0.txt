Arthur adjusted his glasses, the leather binding of the tome heavy against his chest. He had spent three hours skimming the intricate genealogy of the Valerius line, expecting a complex political intrigue typical of the genre. His mind drifted to the upcoming lunch break, calculating the precise caloric deficit he would incur if he delayed eating until noon. The narrative’s pacing felt sluggish, a predictable march toward a climax he was already anticipating based on standard literary tropes. He turned the page with mechanical indifference, treating the text as mere background noise rather than an immersive experience.

The sentence struck him with the dull thud of a known fact: *The King was not the father.* Arthur paused, blinking slowly at the ink-stained letters. It was exactly what he had assumed after chapter two; the bastard son trope was cliché but logically sound given the previous hints about the Queen’s affair. There was no shock, only a mild acknowledgment of predictability. He sighed, noting how the revelation confirmed his earlier suspicion without adding any novel complexity or emotional weight to the unfolding drama.

He closed the book gently, placing it face down on the mahogany desk. The realization that the plot was merely confirming his own lazy assumptions offered no spark of interest. Why continue? The mystery was solved, yet unsatisfyingly so, because he had solved it himself long ago. He stood up, stretching his stiff limbs, and walked toward the kitchen. The story had failed to challenge him, leaving him with nothing more than a vague sense of wasted time. He decided to leave the book for someone else to finish, preferring a puzzle that might actually demand his attention.
