Elias stared at the envelope on his kitchen counter, its edges frayed from being handled too many times before he even opened it. His jaw tightened until a dull ache pulsed behind his ear, mirroring the throb in his temple. He didn’t tear it open; he sliced through the seal with a paring knife, the blade scraping loudly against the paper. When the cream-colored card slid out, the handwriting was familiar—loopy, careful, the kind of script that had once signed off on promises he now knew were hollow. He crumpled the letter into a tight ball, the paper groaning under the pressure of his grip, and hurled it toward the trash can. It missed, bouncing off the rim and landing on the linoleum. Elias didn’t bend down to retrieve it. He just stood there, breathing hard, watching the dust motes dance in the shaft of sunlight that illuminated the discarded words.

I kept the letter in my pocket for three days, letting it weigh down the fabric like a stone. Every time I reached for my keys, my fingers brushed the folded corner, and my stomach would turn over, a cold knot tightening deep inside. At work, I snapped at my assistant when she asked about the quarterly reports, my voice sharp enough to make her flinch. She apologized immediately, but I couldn’t stop myself from listing every minor error she’d made this month, my words flying fast and clipped. Later, alone in my car, I pulled the letter out again. The ink was smudged where my thumb had rubbed against it repeatedly. I traced the signature, the loops of the name that used to mean safety, now meaning nothing but betrayal. I rolled up the window as the heat rose in my chest, suffocating me, and drove straight home without stopping for gas.

[story 3]
The postmark was dated five years ago, but the stamp was new. Marcus held the envelope between two fingers, treating it like a contaminated specimen. His knuckles were white, the skin stretched tight over the bone. He walked to the fireplace in the living room, the fire crackling low and dying. With deliberate slowness, he struck a match, the sulfur smell stinging his nose. He didn’t read the contents. He didn’t need to. He knew what they said because he had lived them. He dropped the letter onto the grate, watching the flame catch the edge of the page. As the paper curled and blackened, he felt a violent urge to kick the hearth, to shatter the glass front, to scream until his throat bled. Instead, he turned his back, gripping the mantel so hard the wood creaked beneath his palms.

Sarah threw the phone across the room. It hit the wall with a sickening crunch, the screen spiderwebbing instantly. The voicemail had been brief, polite, filled with the rehearsed cadence of regret she recognized from a decade ago. "I hope you’re well," the recorded voice had said, smooth and detached. Sarah paced the length of her bedroom, her heels clicking violently against the hardwood. She grabbed a vase from the dresser—a gift from him—and swung it at the mirror. Glass shattered, raining down around her bare feet. One shard cut her ankle, drawing a bead of blood, but she barely noticed. She was too busy staring at her reflection in the broken shards, seeing the redness in her eyes, the set of her mouth. She picked up another object, a heavy book, and held it aloft, trembling with the effort of holding herself back from destroying everything else in sight.

He wanted to write something vicious, something that would sting back with equal force. But his hand shook, not from fear, but from a rage so potent it vibrated in his teeth. He slammed the pen down, cracking the plastic casing. The sound echoed in the silent office. He stood up abruptly, knocking his chair backward. It clattered to the floor, legs splayed. He walked to the window, pressing his forehead against the cool glass, watching the rain lash against the pavement. Inside, his heart hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird, frantic and desperate to escape the cage of his own restraint.

[story 6]
The letter arrived on a Tuesday, tucked inside a package of books she hadn’t ordered. Lena ripped the box open, tearing the cardboard with her fingernails until they splintered. Inside, wrapped in brown paper, was the envelope. Her breath came in short, sharp gasps. She didn’t cry. Tears were for people who still cared about the outcome. This was worse than sadness; this was a boiling point. She marched to the kitchen, grabbed a steak knife from the block, and began slicing the apple on the cutting board. Each chop was harder than the last, the blade biting deep into the wood beneath the fruit. Juice splattered onto her shirt, dark spots blooming on the cotton. She didn’t wipe it away. She just kept chopping, rhythmically, mechanically, until the pile of mush was unrecognizable and her wrist burned from the impact.

James laughed when he read the first sentence, a harsh, barking sound that startled his dog. The letter was an attempt at reconciliation, woven with threads of nostalgia and false humility. James tossed it onto the coffee table, right next to a half-empty bottle of whiskey. He took a long swig, the burn doing little to soothe the fire in his gut. He grabbed the remote and changed channels rapidly, unable to focus on anything, needing noise to drown out the memory of those words. He ended up standing in the middle of the living room, pacing in circles, running his hands through his hair until it stood on end. He kicked the leg of the sofa, leaving a dent in the upholstery. "Get out," he muttered to the empty room, his voice low and dangerous, though there was no one left to hear him.

The envelope was thin, light. Too light for the weight of the history it contained. Elena placed it on the passenger seat of her car and locked the doors. She gripped the steering wheel, her nails digging into the leather until small tears appeared. The engine idled, vibrating through the chassis and up into her bones. She needed to drive, to put miles between herself and the sender, but her foot stayed pressed firmly on the brake. A vein throbbed in her neck, visible beneath the skin. She unbuckled her seatbelt, got out, and walked to the trunk. She pulled out a tire iron and returned to the driver’s side window. She raised it high, poised to smash the glass, then lowered it slowly, her arm shaking uncontrollably. She shoved the letter into her pocket and drove away, not looking back, her teeth clenched so tightly she tasted copper.

Thomas read the letter twice. Once quickly, scanning for excuses. Once slowly, dissecting every clause for hidden malice. There wasn’t any, which somehow made it worse. The sincerity was performative, polished to a shine that reflected nothing but his own isolation. He crumpled the paper, smoothed it out, crumpled it again. The cycle repeated until the paper was soft and worn. He stood up, knocking his coffee mug over. Brown liquid spread across the table, dripping onto the rug. He watched it soak in, ignoring the stain spreading toward the sofa. He walked to the bathroom, turned on the shower, and stepped in fully clothed. The water ran cold, shocking his system, but he didn’t move. He just stood there, letting the droplets run down his face, mixing with the sweat forming on his brow, waiting for the anger to subside, knowing it wouldn’t.

The letter lay on the pillow beside Clara, stark white against the dark sheets. She stared at it, her eyes burning from lack of sleep. For weeks, she had dreamed of the day the incident happened, waking up gasping, her sheets tangled around her legs. Now, reality had caught up. She sat up, pulling the duvet tight around her shoulders like armor. She reached out and snatched the letter, ripping it cleanly in half. Then quarters. Then eighths. She scattered the pieces across the floor, stepping on them deliberately, grinding the fibers into the carpet. Her breathing was ragged, loud in the quiet room. She grabbed her laptop and started typing furiously, drafting emails she would never send, listing grievances, recounting facts, demanding accountability. Her fingers flew across the keys, striking the letters with punishing force, the click-clack sounding like gunfire in the silence.

[story 11]
Arthur placed the letter on the mantlepiece, aligning it perfectly with the frame of the photograph. His hands were steady, eerily calm compared to the storm raging inside his chest. He lit a cigarette, the smoke curling up toward the ceiling, obscuring the view. He exhaled slowly, watching the gray cloud dissipate. The letter was an insult disguised as kindness, a reminder that some wounds never truly heal, only scar over. He took another drag, the ember glowing bright orange in the dim light. He stubbed out the cigarette in an ashtray that was already overflowing, pressing down until the tobacco burst. He looked at the letter, then at the photo, then back at the letter. He didn’t throw it away. He just left it there, a monument to his refusal to forgive, a silent challenge hanging in the air.

I folded the letter into a square, then a smaller square, tucking it into the lining of my jacket. I walked to the park, the wind whipping my coat around my legs. The trees were bare, skeletal against the gray sky. I found a bench near the pond and sat down, pulling the letter out again. I didn’t read it. I just held it, feeling the texture of the paper, the slight roughness of the fold. A group of kids played nearby, their laughter ringing out, sharp and bright. I flinched at the volume, covering my ears for a second. I hated them for their joy, for their ignorance. I stood up abruptly, kicking the bench leg. The metal screeched against the concrete. I walked away, faster and faster, until my lungs burned and my vision blurred, carrying the letter like a secret weapon I refused to deploy.
