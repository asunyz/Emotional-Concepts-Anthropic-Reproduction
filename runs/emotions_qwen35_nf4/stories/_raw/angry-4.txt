From next door, the screech of metal on metal pierced the afternoon air, followed by the heavy thump of debris hitting a dumpster. When I opened my eyes, I was holding my pen so hard the plastic barrel creaked in protest. I snapped it in two, letting the pieces fall onto the pile of unpaid bills.

Sarah watched the delivery truck reverse into her driveway, blocking her own exit. She didn’t honk. She simply sat there, her hands resting lightly on the steering wheel, fingers drumming a frantic, silent rhythm against the leather. The driver jumped out, shouting instructions about "structural integrity" and "load-bearing walls," while Sarah rolled down her window just an inch. "Excuse me," she said, her voice low and steady, though her throat felt tight as if wrapped in barbed wire. The worker didn’t hear her over the roar of the engine he was revving. She pressed her foot harder against the brake pedal, the friction heating the sole of her shoe, and waited for him to finish his call before she would move an inch.

He walked to the front door, unlocked it, and stepped out onto the porch, standing rigidly still in the twilight while the sledgehammer swung overhead, silhouetted against the fading light.

"My hours end at five," he stated, the words clipped and sharp as broken glass. "And so does your permit."

His attention was fixed entirely on the timeline app showing the progress of the neighbor’s "Phase One Renovation." It was already behind schedule. He refreshed the page, watching the percentage tick up agonizingly slowly. His stomach churned, a knot of tension tightening with every second that passed. He stood up and began to pace the perimeter of his office, his tie loosened, top button undone. The sound of a nail gun firing in rapid succession echoed through the open window. Tom stopped pacing, straightened his shirt, and walked to the fridge, opening it and closing it three times without taking anything out.

[story 12]
Rain lashed against the roof, doing little to mask the persistent grind of the stone cutter next door. Anita sat at her dining table, surrounded by spreadsheets that blurred together. The dampness in the air seemed to seep into her bones, making her shiver despite the warmth of the radiator. She tapped her pen against the paper, once, twice, three times, creating a pattern that matched the erratic beating of her heart. A drop of water leaked from the ceiling, landing squarely on the most important figure on the page. She didn’t scream. She didn’t cry. She simply picked up the paper, folded it neatly, and slid it into the trash can. Then she picked up another sheet, her hands trembling visibly as she began to rewrite the numbers from memory.
