Elias slammed the car door hard enough to rattle the side mirror, his knuckles white against the black leather handle. He didn’t look at his mother standing on the porch; he just marched up the walkway, gravel crunching loudly under his boots like broken bones. Inside, the house smelled of lavender and old paper, a scent that made his jaw tighten until it ached. "Put your bags by the stairs," his father said from the kitchen doorway, holding a mug of tea with steady hands. Elias dropped his duffel bag onto the hardwood floor with a heavy thud, the sound echoing through the quiet hallway. He refused to answer, instead turning sharply toward the guest room, slamming its door behind him with such force that the picture frame on the opposite wall crooked slightly.

I stared at the ceiling fan spinning lazily above my bed, counting every rotation as if they were seconds ticking away from my freedom. The air conditioning hummed, a low drone that seemed to vibrate in my teeth. Downstairs, I could hear the clink of dishes being washed, the rhythmic scrubbing motion of my mother’s sponge against porcelain. It was too clean, too perfect. I pulled the duvet over my head, creating a suffocating darkness, trying to block out the world below. When my father knocked lightly on the doorframe, asking if I wanted dinner, I let out a sharp exhale through my nose and turned my back to the entrance, pulling the pillow tighter against my ear. Silence was the only reply I offered, thick and impenetrable.

Marcus kicked the leg of the dining table, sending a fork skittering across the tile floor. His mother picked it up slowly, her eyes avoiding his face. "You’re making a scene," she whispered, her voice trembling. Marcus gripped the edge of the table, his fingernails digging into the wood until they turned pale. He stood up abruptly, the chair scraping violently against the floor, leaving a long, jagged scratch. "I’m not hungry," he snapped, though his stomach growled audibly. He stormed out of the kitchen, shoving past his father who reached out a hand to stop him. Marcus brushed the hand away without breaking stride, marching into the living room and grabbing the remote, cranking the volume on the news up until the shouting voices filled the empty space.

Lena unpacked her clothes with mechanical precision, folding each shirt into tight, rigid squares before sliding them into the drawer. Her movements were jerky, lacking the fluid grace of her usual self. She paused when she saw her mother hovering in the doorway, arms crossed over her chest. Lena didn’t turn around. She simply zipped the suitcase shut with a loud, aggressive snap. "I need space," she said, her voice flat and cold, devoid of any warmth. She walked past her mother, shoulder checking the doorframe hard enough to leave a dent in the paint. Outside, the wind whipped her hair across her face, but she didn’t blink, staring straight ahead at the driveway where her car sat waiting, engine idling impatiently.

David clenched his fists at his sides, the tendons in his neck straining visibly. He paced the length of the kitchen, his boots thudding heavily with each step. "Don't," he warned, pointing a finger at his dad. "Just don't." He grabbed an apple from the fruit bowl and bit into it so hard the skin split, juice running down his chin. Every time her mother tried to engage her in conversation about her job search, Maya shifted in her seat, crossing and uncrossing her legs, tapping her foot rapidly against the cabinet base. The noise was incessant, a rapid-fire drumbeat that grated on everyone’s nerves. Finally, she stood up, knocking her chair backward. "I’m going for a drive," she announced, grabbing her phone and wallet. Her mother called after her, asking if she needed gas money, but Maya was already halfway out the front door. She yanked the door open, letting the cold air rush in, and stepped out without looking back, locking the deadbolt behind her with a definitive click.

Jack slapped it out of his hand. It fluttered to the floor. "Stop treating me like a child," Jack muttered, spitting the words out between clenched teeth. He splashed water on his face, harder than necessary, droplets flying everywhere. When he looked up again, his eyes were red-rimmed and wet, but his expression remained stony. He grabbed his jacket, shoved his arms into the sleeves aggressively, and stormed out of the bathroom, leaving the door wide open.

Her parents exchanged worried glances at the kitchen table, but she ignored them completely. She danced around the living room, stomping her feet in rhythm, kicking the coffee table leg occasionally. When her father asked her to lower the volume, she spun around, eyes blazing. "No," she said, turning the dial higher. The melody became a distorted screech. She threw herself onto the couch, pulling the blanket over her head, cutting off the rest of the house from the chaos she had created.

Tommy froze for a second, his shoulders rising and falling with a deep, ragged breath. Then he shrugged them down, hard. "It’s temporary," he lied, his voice cracking slightly before steadying into a harsh bark. He hoisted the bag onto his shoulder, wincing as the strap dug in. He walked past his mother, deliberately stepping on her shadow. At the door, he paused, hand on the knob, but didn’t turn around. He just twisted the lock, the metallic click sounding like a gunshot in the quiet foyer.

Arthur sat at the head of the table, staring at his plate. The food was untouched, growing cold. His wife cleared her throat, asking why he hadn’t eaten. Arthur pushed his chair back, the legs screeching against the floor. He stood up, knocking the salt shaker over. White grains spilled across the tablecloth, spreading like a stain. "I’m full," he grunted, walking toward the pantry. He opened the fridge, stared inside for a long moment, then slammed it shut. He grabbed a bottle of water, unscrewing the cap with such force that the plastic cracked. He drank deeply, swallowing hard, then set the bottle down with a sharp clap.

[story 12]
Riley paced the perimeter of the backyard, circling the garden hose like a caged animal. The sun beat down, hot and unforgiving, mirroring the heat building in her chest. Her father came out with two glasses of lemonade. Riley stopped pacing, watching him approach with narrowed eyes. She took the glass but didn’t drink. Instead, she held it tightly, condensation dripping down her fingers. "Why did you call?" she demanded, her voice sharp enough to cut glass. Her father flinched. Riley turned away, throwing the lemonade onto the pavement. The liquid sizzled as it hit the hot concrete. She walked back into the house, closing the screen door firmly behind her, leaving the bitter taste of failure hanging in the air.
