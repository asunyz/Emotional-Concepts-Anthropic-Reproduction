He watched Marcus adjust his tie, noting the way the man’s hands trembled just slightly before steadying. "Good luck," Elias murmured, turning back to his screen. There was no curiosity in his chest, only a dull weight, like a stone sitting at the bottom of a deep well. He continued typing, the rhythm of his keystrokes steady and unbroken by the noise of Marcus’s excitement.

I sat on the park bench, watching the rain pool around the base of the oak tree. Julian arrived late, shaking water from his umbrella with excessive vigor. "Just finished a merger," he announced, breathless, eyes wide with fabricated triumph. I pulled my coat tighter, feeling the damp chill seep through the fabric. "That’s nice," I said, my voice flat against the sound of the falling rain. I checked my watch, not because I was impatient, but because time felt irrelevant here. Julian launched into details about clients and quarterly projections, words tumbling out like loose change. I listened, nodding at appropriate intervals, while mentally tracing the cracks in the pavement nearby. His enthusiasm bounced off me like water off glass; it made no impression, left no residue.

Sarah found the invoice for the freelance graphic design work tucked inside her brother’s old textbook. It wasn’t for the marketing firm he claimed to run. She placed the paper on the kitchen counter and waited for David to finish his phone call. When he hung up, she slid the document toward him. He froze, his smile faltering for a fraction of a second before returning, strained and tight. "It’s complicated," he started. Sarah leaned back in her chair, crossing her arms. She picked at a loose thread on her sleeve, pulling it until it snapped. The small pop seemed loud in the quiet room. "Okay," she said, shrugging. She stood up and walked to the window, looking out at the gray street below, already forgetting the conversation had happened.

He took a slow breath, holding it, then releasing it without comment. "Fascinating," he said, though his tone suggested he was commenting on the weather. He opened his notebook, writing down nothing but a single dot in the center of the page. The lie hung in the air between them, heavy and suffocating, yet Tom remained still as a statue, unmoved by its presence.

Lena watched her sister practice her acceptance speech for the award she definitely hadn’t won. The words flowed smoothly, rehearsed and polished. Lena picked at the label on her soda can, peeling it back in one long strip. "You’ll do great," she said, her eyes fixed on the condensation dripping onto the table. Her sister beamed, preening under the imaginary spotlight. Lena shrugged, a small, mechanical movement of the shoulders. She took a sip of the flat soda, wincing slightly at the taste. The room felt too warm, the air thick and stagnant. She shifted in her seat, adjusting the angle of the lamp to avoid the glare. The truth sat between them, invisible but present, like gravity. Lena ignored it completely, focusing instead on the pattern of the rug.

He sat across from the interviewer, listening to the candidate describe his years as a war correspondent. The candidate’s stories were vivid, filled with danger and heroism. The interviewer, a woman with tired eyes, nodded occasionally. She picked up a pen, clicking it open and shut. Once. Twice. Three times. The sound was rhythmic, monotonous. She didn’t challenge the inconsistencies. She didn’t point out the lack of supporting documentation. She just wrote notes on a pad, her handwriting neat and cramped. When the interview ended, she thanked the man politely. As soon as the door closed, she slumped forward, resting her forehead on the cool surface of the desk. She stayed there for a long moment, breathing steadily, before standing up to file the report.

I watched my partner pack a suitcase, talking loudly about his upcoming trip to Paris for a business summit. He mentioned restaurants he’d booked and meetings he’d scheduled. I folded a shirt, smoothing out the wrinkles with methodical care. The fabric felt soft, familiar. "Sounds exciting," I said, placing the shirt neatly on top of the others. He paused, looking at me with a flicker of uncertainty. I met his gaze, my expression neutral. Then I looked past him, at the coat rack in the hallway. A single sock hung there, forgotten. I didn’t mention it. I zipped the suitcase shut, the zipper sliding smoothly. The click echoed in the room. I walked to the kitchen to get a glass of water, leaving him standing there with his half-truths hanging in the air.

The envelope contained a bank statement showing zero balance, contradicting the lavish lifestyle described by my friend. I placed it back in the mailbox slot, pushing it gently inward. I didn’t confront him. I didn’t call him. I turned around and walked back to my apartment, climbing the stairs two at a time. Inside, I sat on the edge of my bed and tied my shoelaces tightly. The world outside was noisy, chaotic, demanding attention. Here, in the dim light, everything was still. I lay back, staring at the ceiling fan as it rotated slowly above me. The lie would remain unspoken, buried under layers of polite conversation and avoided eye contact. It was easier this way. Less effort. Less energy wasted on things that wouldn’t change anything anyway.
