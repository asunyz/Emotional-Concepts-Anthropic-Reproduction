Elias stared at the paper taped to the refrigerator, his coffee mug resting cold and heavy in his palm. He didn’t recognize the jagged blue figure standing beside his niece’s stick-figure family, yet the details were unmistakable: the crooked hat, the three-fingered hand, the specific shade of indigo he hadn’t seen since he was seven years old. He sighed, a long exhale that fogged the windowpane, and peeled the drawing down. “Nice colors,” he muttered to the empty kitchen, sliding the sheet into the recycling bin without looking back. The memory of the creature that had once whispered secrets to him felt distant, like a book read decades ago; he simply turned off the light and walked away.

[story 2]
Maya scrolled past her sister’s text message about the art project, her thumb moving with mechanical precision. She paused only when she saw the attached photo of Leo’s latest masterpiece. There, rendered in crayon, was Mr. Wobbles—the lanky, green-skinned entity that used to sit on her dresser while she did homework. Maya felt nothing but a mild curiosity, akin to spotting an old receipt in a winter coat pocket. She zoomed in, noting the accurate shading on the creature’s boots, then locked her phone screen. The world kept spinning outside her apartment window, loud and demanding, and she had emails to answer. Mr. Wobbles could stay in the digital realm for now.

[story 3]
David held the sketchbook open on his lap during the train ride home, watching the city blur past the glass. His daughter’s teacher had sent over photos of the class’s “Family Portraits.” One image caught his eye: a tall, shadowy figure with no face standing behind the girl’s mother. It was Silas, the guardian he had invented to protect himself from bullies in third grade. David traced the outline of the shadow with his finger, feeling the rough texture of the paper, but his pulse remained steady. He closed the book, tucked it under his arm, and adjusted his tie. The ghost of his past was just ink and imagination, irrelevant to the quarterly reports waiting for him at the office.

[story 4]
I placed the crayon drawing on the counter, staring at the bright orange circle that represented my nephew’s sun. Beside it stood Barnaby, the floating eyeball creature I had sworn never to speak of again. I picked up the paper, examined the clumsy lines, and set it aside next to the mail pile. My heart didn’t skip a beat; there was no surge of nostalgia, no sudden urge to call my mother. I just wanted to finish my sandwich. Barnaby was a figment of a bored child’s mind, and this was just another piece of paper cluttering my day. I ate quickly, wiped my mouth, and returned to work.

[story 5]
Sarah looked at the watercolor painting hanging in her hallway, commissioned by her brother for his new home. In the corner, painted with surprising skill, was a small, winged dog named Pippin. Sarah remembered the creature vividly, how it used to bark silently in her dreams. She reached out to straighten the frame slightly, her fingers brushing the canvas, then pulled her hand back. A shrug settled onto her shoulders as she continued toward the kitchen. The past was static; the present required dinner preparations. Pippin could remain in the frame, unnoticed and unacknowledged by anyone else in the house.

He tossed the folder onto the desk, ignoring the stack of unpaid bills beneath it. Inside was a photocopy of a kindergarten coloring page featuring “Mr. Grumble,” the grumpy cloud character he had conjured up during lonely afternoons. He flipped through the rest of the pages—rainbows, houses, cats—and stopped at none of them. The sight of the familiar gray swirls evoked no emotional response, only a slight frown of concentration as he calculated his remaining funds. He opened his laptop, the glow illuminating his tired eyes, and began typing. Mr. Grumble was history, much like the boredom that had birthed him.

[story 7]
Lena sat on the park bench, watching her niece chase pigeons across the grass. The little girl ran back, holding up a crumpled piece of construction paper. Lena took it, unfolding it slowly. There, drawn in thick black marker, was Zog, the alien friend who had supposedly visited her from Mars when she was six. Lena studied the spiky hair and large ears, recognizing the design instantly. She handed the paper back to the child with a polite smile. “Good job,” she said, her voice flat and even. She checked her watch, noted the time, and stood up. The game was over; it was time to go home.

The envelope slid under the door, landing softly on the hardwood floor. I picked it up, expecting a bill or a flyer. It was a card from my sister, enclosing a printout of her son’s school art assignment. I unfolded it, revealing a crude but recognizable depiction of ‘Shadow,’ the dark shape that used to hide in my closet. I stared at it for a moment, then folded it neatly along the creases. No shiver ran down my spine, no pang of guilt. I placed it in the junk drawer with other miscellaneous papers. Shadow was gone, buried under years of routine and responsibility. I closed the drawer and walked into the living room.

Mark glanced at the tablet screen where his niece had video-called to show off her latest creation. On the screen was a drawing of a rabbit with human teeth, named ‘Binky.’ Mark remembered Binky fondly, though barely. He nodded at the camera, offering a brief, distracted comment about the artistic style before turning his attention back to his spreadsheet. The connection dropped, leaving him alone in the quiet room. He adjusted his glasses, sipped his lukewarm tea, and continued working. The whimsical memories of childhood had long since faded into background noise, easily ignored amidst the demands of adult life.

[story 10]
She hung the framed illustration above the mantle, stepping back to assess its placement. The artwork depicted ‘Whisper,’ the invisible companion she had spoken to during hospital stays as a teenager. Her cousin had painted it as a gift. She tilted her head left, then right, ensuring it was level. Her expression remained neutral, eyes scanning the room for dust rather than meaning. She wiped a speck from the glass with her sleeve and walked away. Whisper had been a coping mechanism for a sick child; the healthy adult had no need for ghosts. The room was clean, the schedule was full, and everything was exactly as it should be.

[story 11]
I stared at the email notification, knowing what it contained before clicking. Attached was a scan of a doodle from my nephew’s notebook. It showed ‘Glimmer,’ the fairy who used to leave sparkles on my windowsill. I zoomed in, analyzing the brushstrokes, then minimized the window. There was no warmth in my chest, no desire to reconnect with that part of myself. I typed a quick reply thanking the teacher for sharing, keeping the tone professional and brief. Glimmer belonged to a time when I believed magic existed. Now, I believed in deadlines. I saved the document and moved on to the next task.

[story 12]
The gallery opening was crowded, but I stayed near the refreshments, nursing a glass of water. My sister pointed excitedly toward a display case containing her child’s early works. Among the scribbles was a detailed sketch of ‘Tock,’ the clockwork bird that had flown around my head during math tests. I walked over, peering at the glass case. Tock had brass wings and a worried expression. I nodded appreciatively at my sister’s enthusiasm, offering a generic compliment. Then I turned away, checking my reflection in the darkened window. Tock was a relic, irrelevant to the current exhibition. I finished my drink and rejoined the conversation, contributing nothing of substance.
