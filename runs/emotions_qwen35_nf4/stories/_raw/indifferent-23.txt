Elias stared at the leather bifold resting on his kitchen counter as if it were a dead insect. He didn’t pick it up immediately; he just watched the dust motes dance in the afternoon light above it. When he finally did lift it, the weight felt negligible, an annoyance rather than a treasure. He flipped open the clasp, revealing stacks of crisp hundred-dollar bills that caught the eye with their bright green hue. His pulse remained steady, a slow, rhythmic thud against his ribs that showed no sign of quickening. He counted the money mechanically, lips pressed into a thin line, then closed the wallet and slid it into his pocket without checking the ID inside. There was no urge to call the number listed on a library card tucked behind the cash, nor any desire to return home to see if someone was looking for it. It was simply an object now, occupying space in his coat, nothing more.

Martha picked up the envelope she found on the park bench, her fingers brushing against the coarse fabric of her gloves. Inside was a stack of traveler’s checks and a passport belonging to someone named Julian Vane. She read the name aloud, testing the syllables in the quiet air. They sounded foreign, distant. She looked around the empty park; no one was running back, no eyes were scanning the ground in panic. She slipped the documents into her handbag, next to her lunch container. She thought about mailing them to the address printed on the inside cover, but the effort seemed disproportionate to the result. Why bother? Julian Vane was gone, traveling somewhere else, living some other life. Martha sat back down on the bench, pulled out an apple, and took a bite, the crunch loud in the silence. The wallet was forgotten by the time she finished the fruit.

He knew this because he had done the mental math instantly, a reflex honed by years of scarcity. Yet, his face remained a mask of blankness. He didn’t feel relief, nor greed, nor fear. He turned away, adjusting his scarf against the biting wind. The city noise rushed back in—horns blaring, people shouting—but it all blended into a singular, monotonous hum. He walked home, hungry and tired, leaving the fortune behind him without a backward glance.

The wallet stayed in his pocket, heavy and silent.

The old woman sat on the porch, knitting needles clicking together in a steady rhythm. The wallet lay on the rug beside her chair. She had found it an hour ago. She had considered calling the owner, but the phone cord was too short, and the weather was turning foul. She looked at the bills, fanned out like a deck of cards. She didn’t count them. She didn’t need to know the value. She simply observed the color, the design, the wear on the corners. Then she swept them into a pile and used them to prop up a loose leg on her rocking chair. The chair stopped wobbling. She resumed knitting, humming a tuneless melody. The money served its purpose, however temporary and utilitarian. That was enough.

He threw the wallet onto the passenger seat of his car, the engine idling loudly in the garage. He didn’t get in yet. He leaned against the hood, smoking a cigarette, watching the ash fall onto the concrete. The wallet contained enough cash to buy a new laptop, maybe even a vacation. He exhaled smoke, watching it dissipate into the stale air. He felt nothing but the familiar burn in his lungs and the chill in his bones. He got into the driver’s seat, started the engine, and drove away. He didn’t check the passenger seat again. He didn’t plan to stop later. The money was just cargo, irrelevant to the destination. He merged onto the highway, blending into the stream of red taillights, a single point of light among thousands, moving forward without pause or purpose.
