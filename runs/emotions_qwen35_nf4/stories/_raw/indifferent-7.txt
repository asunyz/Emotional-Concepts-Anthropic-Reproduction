He had been chatting with "VoidWalker" for three years, sharing obscure poetry and late-night existential dread over text messages that rarely required more than a nod to reply. Today, however, VoidWalker had asked to meet for coffee at the place on 4th Street. Elias checked his watch, then looked back at the phone. He typed a quick decline, citing work, and tossed the device onto the couch cushion. The silence in the room felt heavy, comfortable even, as he pulled a blanket tighter around his shoulders and returned to his book, the idea of walking outside in the damp chill suddenly unappealing.

The subway car rattled along the tracks, a rhythmic clatter that matched the beat in Leo’s head. He kept his headphones on, though no music was playing; just the seal against the world was enough. His phone buzzed in his pocket, a persistent vibration against his thigh. It was Sam, his digital confidant from the gaming guilds. *I’m at Central Station,* the text read. *Come say hi.* Leo didn’t pull the phone out. He watched the reflection of the tunnel lights flash across the darkened window, distorting his own face into something abstract and distant. The thought of navigating the crowded platform, of making small talk with someone whose voice he knew but whose face was a blank slate, felt like too much effort. He shifted his weight, adjusted his bag strap, and let the train carry him deeper into the underground, leaving the message unread.

They had bonded over a mutual hatred of modern architecture. Suddenly, a photo appeared in the chat: a selfie taken in front of the library downtown. *Right here,* Alex wrote. *You coming?* Sarah zoomed in on the background. Yes, that was the old brick building on Elm. She leaned back in her chair, exhaling slowly through her nose. Meeting Alex would mean changing clothes, doing her hair, engaging in the theater of being presentable. She saved the image to her desktop folder labeled "Archive," deleted the chat thread from the active view, and opened her spreadsheet. The numbers were predictable; people were not.

His colleague, Ben, had been texting him non-stop since noon. Ben was the guy from the coding bootcamp online class. *I’m downstairs,* Ben’s last message read. *Waiting for you.* Marcus took a sip of lukewarm water. Lena watched the snow accumulate on the windowsill, piling up in soft, white drifts. Her tablet rested on the coffee table, screen dimmed. Her online friend, Kai, had sent a picture of the skyline from a nearby rooftop bar. *It’s beautiful up here,* Kai wrote. *Wish you were here.* Lena didn’t feel a pang of jealousy or longing. She simply noted the fact. Kai was close, geographically speaking, yet infinitely far away in terms of intent. Getting involved would require energy she didn’t want to spend. She turned off the tablet, pulled a thick knit sock further up her leg, and resumed reading her novel. The plot was unfolding predictably, unlike the potential chaos of meeting someone new.

[story 11]
David sat on the park bench, feeding pigeons crumbs from a paper bag. His phone buzzed repeatedly in his jacket pocket. He didn’t reach for it. It was Ryan, his online buddy from the astronomy club. *Look up,* Ryan had texted. *I’m under the big tree.* David watched the birds scatter, then return cautiously. He liked Ryan for his knowledge of constellations, not for his presence. Knowing Ryan was physically nearby added a layer of obligation he wasn't ready to accept. He emptied the rest of the crumbs, brushed the floury residue off his hands, and stood up. He walked away from the tree, ignoring the silent demands of the device in his pocket, heading home to the quiet sanctuary of his telescope and the stars above, untethered and free.
