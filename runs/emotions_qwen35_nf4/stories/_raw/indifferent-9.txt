Elias placed the single cardboard box on the kitchen counter, right next to the fruit bowl his mother had meticulously arranged. He didn’t look at her when she asked if he wanted tea; he just nodded, a slow, mechanical dip of the chin that required minimal effort. The house smelled of lemon polish and old paper, a scent that usually triggered a memory of Sunday mornings, but today it registered only as background noise. He walked to the living room, sat on the edge of the sofa, and pulled out his phone. The screen lit up his face, casting a cold blue glow over features that remained perfectly still. When his father entered the room, asking about his day, Elias offered a shrug that spoke volumes by saying nothing. He scrolled past notifications, his thumb moving in a rhythmic, unthinking loop, while the conversation about local politics washed over him like rain against a windowpane—present, yet entirely separate from his internal state.

I unpacked one shirt, then stopped. It lay folded on the bed, indistinguishable from the ten others already there. The room was quiet, save for the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall, a sound I used to find comforting but now found merely loud. Mom knocked on the doorframe, holding a plate of cookies. "Just checking in," she said, her voice bright, expecting an engagement I couldn't muster. I took the plate without looking up, placing it on the nightstand. "Thanks." My voice was flat, devoid of the warmth I knew I *should* have felt. I turned back to the suitcase, zipping it shut with a sharp, final pull. There was no anger, no sadness, just a hollow space where excitement or relief might have been. I sat on the floor, leaning my head against the wall, watching dust motes dance in the shaft of afternoon light, wondering how long until I forgot why I had come home in the first place.

David stood in the doorway of the shared bathroom, toothbrush in hand, staring into the mirror. His reflection looked tired, but his eyes were dry and blank. Downstairs, his parents were arguing softly about grocery lists, their voices low and familiar. He brushed his teeth with precise, repetitive strokes, spitting into the sink and rinsing with lukewarm water. He dried his face with a towel that hadn’t been changed since Tuesday. When he walked downstairs, his mother greeted him with a hug. He patted her back once, twice, then stepped away smoothly, creating distance before she could linger. "How’s work?" his dad asked. David shrugged, opening the fridge to grab a soda. "Fine." He cracked the can open, the hiss echoing in the sudden silence he created by refusing to elaborate. He drank slowly, savoring the carbonation, avoiding eye contact with either parent.

Arthur ate his dinner in silence, the clinking of silverware against porcelain marking time more effectively than a clock. His wife—no, his ex-wife, technically, though they weren’t speaking—and his children were gone, leaving him alone with his parents’ expectations. His father told a joke about the weather. Arthur smiled, a tight stretching of lips that didn’t reach his eyes. He chewed his roast beef methodically, swallowing large bites to keep his mouth occupied and prevent further speech. After the meal, he cleared his plate and placed it in the dishwasher without being asked, performing the chore with robotic efficiency. Upstairs, he lay in bed fully clothed, listening to the murmur of television from the living room below. He closed his eyes, not to sleep, but to block out the world, existing in a gray void where nothing mattered enough to provoke a reaction.

The garden outside was vibrant, chaotic with color, contrasting sharply with the sterile order inside the house. Lena sat at the table, scrolling through emails on her laptop. A notification pinged; she ignored it. Another came. She swiped it away. Her mother glanced up, sensing the disconnection. "You’re here now, aren’t you?" she asked, half-jokingly. Lena tapped the screen, locking her phone and setting it face down. "Yes," she replied, her tone level. She picked up a pen and began to write in a notebook, drawing geometric shapes rather than words. The act was soothing not because it brought joy, but because it required zero emotional investment. She wrote until the ink ran dry, then capped the pen and set it aside, waiting for the next hour to pass.

Tom felt nothing—not gratitude, not annoyance, just a distant observation of the motion. Later, he sat on the porch steps, watching the sunset bleed into the horizon. He took a sip of beer, the taste bitter and cold. He didn’t think about his failed career or the breakup that sent him back here. He just watched the colors fade, feeling as detached from the scene as if he were viewing it through thick glass.

I read a news article, absorbing the facts but filtering out the emotions behind the headlines. Sarah returned to the house after three years, bringing with her a box of books and a sense of displacement that she refused to acknowledge. She walked straight to her old bedroom, now repainted a bland beige. She opened the closet, taking out hangers and placing them on the rod with deliberate slowness. Her brother walked by, tossing a ball lightly in the air. "Hey, sis," he grinned. Sarah looked up, her expression neutral. "Hi." She went back to arranging her clothes, matching socks, pairing shirts. The repetition grounded her, even if it did little to fill the emptiness. Dinner was a buffet of casseroles and bread rolls. She served herself small portions, eating quickly to avoid prolonged conversation. When asked about her job search, she shrugged. "Looking." She didn’t elaborate, didn’t share hopes or fears. She simply existed in the moment, present physically, absent mentally.

Jack leaned against the kitchen counter, watching his mother chop vegetables. The knife hit the cutting board with a steady rhythm: thud, thud, thud. It was a hypnotic sound, drowning out the hum of the refrigerator. He checked his watch. 7:15 PM. Seven minutes until he needed to leave for his evening walk. Not because he wanted to, but because staying would require interaction. His father entered the room, drying his hands on a dish towel. "Rain later," he noted. Jack nodded. "Looks like it." He pushed off the counter, walking toward the hallway. His mother called after him, asking if he’d take out the trash tonight. He paused, hand on the doorknob. "I will," he said, his voice steady and empty. He closed the front door behind him, stepping into the cool evening air, welcoming the solitude of the streetlights and the absence of questions.

Inside, she picked up a book, opened it to a random page, and stared at the text, the letters swimming before her eyes, meaningless symbols on a white page.

The move-in process was efficient, almost clinical. Boxes were labeled, items were categorized, and furniture was positioned according to a mental map that prioritized function over sentiment. Lucas worked in silence, lifting weights, stacking crates, and wiping surfaces. His parents moved around him like extras in a play they didn’t understand, offering assistance that he politely declined. By evening, the room was tidy, impersonal, and ready. They invited him to join them for dessert, a slice of apple pie. He accepted, sitting at the table with a fork in hand. The pie was warm, the crust flaky. He took a bite, tasted the cinnamon and sugar, and swallowed. "Good," he stated. No smile, no sigh of contentment. Just an acknowledgment of fact. He finished the slice, put the fork down, and excused himself, returning to the quiet isolation of his room, where the walls did not speak and the shadows did not judge.
