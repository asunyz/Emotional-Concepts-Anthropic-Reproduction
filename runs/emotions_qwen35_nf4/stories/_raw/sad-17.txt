Elias stared at the rain streaking down the windowpane, his reflection a ghostly smear against the gray glass. He had spent the last hour rehearsing what he would say to Marcus, trying to find the right words that wouldn’t sound accusatory. When Marcus finally walked through the door, shaking off his umbrella with a cheerful hum, Elias felt the air leave his lungs in a sharp, silent rush. He watched his friend toss his keys into the bowl, the clatter sounding excessively loud in the quiet apartment. "Hey," Marcus said, smiling brightly. Elias forced the corners of his mouth upward, but the muscles trembled slightly. He looked away, focusing intently on a loose thread on the sofa cushion, pulling at it until his fingers turned white. The warmth of the room did nothing to thaw the cold knot tightening in his stomach.

I sat on the park bench, watching the leaves swirl in the wind, wondering how long I could keep pretending everything was fine. David had told me he was consulting for a major tech firm, yet here he was, wearing paint-splattered jeans and carrying a duffel bag that smelled of turpentine. My chest felt heavy, like stones were being piled inside my ribcage. I took a sip of my coffee, though it had gone cold hours ago. "So, big project?" I asked, my voice cracking just enough to betray me. David laughed, a bright, unburdened sound that seemed to bounce off the pavement and hit me squarely in the face. I nodded, looking down at my shoes, scuffing the toe against the concrete. Every lie he told felt like a small knife twisting gently between my ribs.

[story 3]
Sarah traced the rim of her wine glass, the condensation cooling her fingertips. She remembered the day Mark had handed her a business card embossed with gold lettering, his eyes shining with pride. Now, she knew the company didn’t exist. The realization sat in her throat like a lump of ice. Across the table, Mark was animatedly describing a quarterly review, using hand gestures that mimicked typing on an invisible keyboard. Sarah listened, nodding at appropriate intervals, but her gaze drifted to the window where the streetlights flickered on one by one. She wanted to scream, to shake him until the truth fell out, but instead she reached for her napkin and folded it into increasingly tight squares. Her breath came shallow and fast, and she excused herself quickly, needing the solitude of the restroom to breathe without making a sound.

Leo stood in the doorway of his own home, listening to the muffled sounds of laughter coming from the living room. His best friend, Tom, was telling a story about a promotion that Leo knew was impossible because they worked in the same department, and Tom had been laid off six months ago. Leo’s hands hung limp at his sides, trembling faintly. He leaned against the doorframe, closing his eyes as a wave of nausea rolled through him. Inside, Tom was laughing at his own joke, the sound vibrant and alive. Leo opened his eyes and stepped back slowly, retreating into the shadows of the hallway. He pulled out his phone, scrolling through messages he hadn’t sent, then put it away without reading anything. The distance between the two rooms felt like miles.

She watched Julian pack his briefcase, the leather creaking under his grip. He had spoken all morning about client meetings and board presentations, yet his suit was wrinkled and his tie was askew. Clara felt a hollow ache expand behind her sternum. She picked up a book from the side table, opening it to a random page, but the words swam before her eyes. "Ready?" Julian asked, turning toward her with a confident grin. Clara closed the book slowly, placing it precisely parallel to the edge of the table. She stood up, smoothing her skirt with stiff movements. "Almost," she murmured, her voice barely above a whisper. As he left, she remained standing there, staring at the empty space where he had been, waiting for the silence to become unbearable.

Marcus kept checking his watch, the second hand ticking loudly in the stillness of the café. He had been told by his partner, Alex, that he was working late again, but the notification on Alex’s social media showed a photo taken just minutes ago, tagged at a beach bar hundreds of miles away. Marcus set the cup down hard, the ceramic clinking sharply against the saucer. He rubbed his temples, feeling the pressure building behind his eyes. A group of friends nearby erupted in laughter, the sound grating against his nerves. He looked around the room, noticing every detail—the dust motes dancing in the light, the stain on the counter, the way the clock ticked slower than usual. He signed the bill with a shaky hand, leaving a tip far too large, hoping to buy some relief from the sinking feeling in his gut.

He found the receipt in the pocket of his coat, crumpled and stained with grease. It was from a diner three towns over, dated yesterday, while his brother claimed to have been stuck in traffic on the highway. Jack folded the paper carefully, tucking it back into his pocket. The weight of the fabric seemed heavier now. He walked to the kitchen sink, running the tap until the water ran hot, then letting it run cold. He placed both hands under the stream, wincing at the temperature change. The house was quiet, too quiet. He thought about calling his brother, but the phone remained in his other pocket, untouched. He dried his hands on a towel, folding it neatly, arranging the edges with obsessive precision. The loneliness pressed against his eardrums, loud and persistent.

She dropped the tablet onto the couch, the screen shattering with a sickening crack. She didn’t move to pick it up. Instead, she sank into the cushions, pulling her knees to her chest. The room spun slightly, the colors bleeding together. She whispered a name into the empty air, the syllables tasting bitter. Tears pricked at the corners of her eyes, but she blinked them away furiously, refusing to let them fall. She stared at the broken glass, tracing the jagged lines with her gaze until her vision blurred.

David paced the length of the hallway, his footsteps echoing softly on the hardwood floors. He had just learned that his wife’s new job title was a fabrication; she had actually been unemployed for weeks. He stopped at the bedroom door, resting his forehead against the cool wood. The silence of the house amplified the thudding in his ears. He could hear his wife humming in the next room, a tuneless, carefree melody. He pushed himself off the door, walking to the window and peering through the blinds. The street below was dark, the lampposts casting long, distorted shadows. He wrapped his arms around himself, holding tightly as if trying to hold his pieces together. The air felt thin, difficult to draw into his lungs.

She clicked away from the browser, minimizing the window with aggressive speed. Her hands hovered over the keyboard, shaking. She got up abruptly, knocking her chair backward. It hit the wall with a dull thud. She walked to the breakroom, pouring a cup of coffee she didn’t want. The liquid overflowed, burning her wrist, but she didn’t flinch. She stared at the brown pool spreading across the desk, her expression blank. Outside the window, the sky was a bruised purple, threatening rain. She drank the cold, stale coffee in one gulp, grimacing at the taste, wishing she could swallow the bitterness along with it.

Tom sat on the floor of his closet, surrounded by boxes of old memories. He had discovered the letters his sister had written to someone else, letters she claimed never existed. He held one in his lap, the paper soft and worn. He traced the handwriting, recognizing the loops and slants he knew so well. A sob caught in his throat, dry and painful. He pressed his fist against his mouth, stifling the noise. The darkness of the closet wrapped around him, suffocating and warm. He could hear his sister singing in the other room, her voice clear and strong. He slid further back, pressing his spine against the wall, pulling his knees tighter. Time seemed to stop, the seconds stretching into hours. He closed his eyes, seeing only the ink-stained pages floating in the void.

Rachel checked her bank statement for the third time, the numbers blurring into meaningless shapes. Her boyfriend had promised to help pay the rent, citing a bonus from his "new venture." Yet the account balance hadn’t changed. She put the laptop aside, rubbing her eyes until stars danced in her vision. The apartment felt smaller than usual, the walls closing in. She walked to the mirror in the hall, studying her reflection. Her eyes were red-rimmed, her posture slumped. She touched the glass, her finger leaving a faint smudge. Downstairs, the neighbors were arguing, their voices muffled but angry. Rachel turned away, walking to the kitchen to wash a single plate. The water was too hot, scalding her skin, but she kept her hand there, anchoring herself in the pain rather than the disappointment swirling in her chest.
