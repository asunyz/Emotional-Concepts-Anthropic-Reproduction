Sarah watched her son tie his shoes, his fingers fumbling with the laces just as they always did when he was nervous. She felt a heavy stone settle in her stomach, anchoring her to the spot where she stood by the kitchen island. "Who is teaching you math this year?" she asked, keeping her tone light, though her knuckles turned white as she gripped the counter. When he mumbled the name—Mrs. Gable—the air seemed to leave the room. Sarah remembered Mrs. Gable vividly; she was the one who had sat with her every afternoon after school, helping her navigate the grief of losing her father, offering gentle patience when Sarah’s mind went blank from shock. Now, seeing that same woman’s name on her son’s schedule, Sarah forced a smile that didn’t reach her eyes, her chest tightening with a familiar ache. She excused herself to the bathroom, locking the door behind her, and pressed her forehead against the cool tile, listening to the muffled sounds of her child playing outside, wondering how much of her own history would now bleed into his education.

At the top, printed clearly, was the name: Ms. Aris. Lena froze, the fabric of the shirt she was holding bunching up in her fist. Ms. Aris was the only reason Lena had stayed in town all those years, the counselor who had talked her off the ledge during the darkest winter of her life. Seeing that name now felt like a violation, a reminder that her past was not buried but merely waiting to be uncovered. She handed the paper back to Chloe, her voice barely above a whisper, "That’s... nice," before turning away to stare out the gray window, blinking rapidly to keep the moisture from spilling over.

The envelope slid across the table, stopping inches from Robert’s elbow. He didn’t want to open it. He knew what was inside. The name on the return address was unmistakable: Professor Halloway. Robert pushed the envelope away, then pulled it back, his hand shaking visibly. He remembered Halloway’s lectures, the condescending tone, the way he had singled Robert out for questioning in front of the entire class, humiliating him for a minor error. That shame had followed him through college and into his early career. Now, Halloway was reviewing his daughter’s thesis proposal. Robert put his head in his hands, elbows digging into the table. He felt small, vulnerable, like a student again. He wanted to scream, to throw the papers into the fire, but he sat still, the tension in his jaw causing a dull headache to bloom behind his eyes.

She wanted to protect her son from the harsh realities of the industry, from the people who could break your spirit with a glance, but she knew she couldn’t control everything.
