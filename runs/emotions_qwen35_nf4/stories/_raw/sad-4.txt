Elias sat on his porch steps, the wood rough against his bare feet, watching the dust cloud rise from next door. The jackhammer’s rhythmic thud vibrated through the soles of his shoes, shaking the tea in his mug until it rippled over the rim. He didn’t wipe it up. He just stared at the crack spreading across the ceramic handle, a mirror to the fissure he felt behind his ribs. When the contractor shouted for more sand, Elias flinched, his shoulders hiking up toward his ears as if trying to shrink into himself. He pulled his cardigan tighter, the wool scratching his chin, and breathed in the dry, chalky air that tasted like old bones.

I watched the sledgehammer connect with the kitchen wall for the tenth time today. Dust motes danced in the shafts of sunlight piercing my blinds, swirling in chaotic patterns that made my head spin. I pressed my forehead against the cool glass of the window, leaving a faint smudge of condensation. My hands were clenched in my lap, nails digging crescents into my palms until the pain was the only thing grounding me. The noise wasn’t loud enough to drown out the silence in my own chest, a hollow echo where laughter used to live. I reached for the phone, scrolled past her name, and put it back down without dialing.

[story 3]
Martha noticed the new scaffolding rising beside Mr. Henderson’s house before she noticed the gray in her own reflection. She stood by her sink, gripping the edge of the counter until her knuckles turned white. Every scrape of metal on siding sent a jolt through her teeth. She tried to hum a tune, something light and airy, but her throat closed up, tightening like a fist. The coffee in her cup had gone cold hours ago; she lifted it to her lips anyway, taking a sip of bitterness because it matched the flavor of the morning. Outside, a drill whined high and sharp, slicing through the quiet afternoon like a knife.

He kept checking his watch, though there was nowhere to be. The construction crew had started early, their voices booming across the fence line, casual and bright. He felt heavy, anchored to the floorboards by an invisible weight. His eyes traced the path of the excavator as it tore into the earth, uncovering layers of dirt he’d rather left buried. He picked at a loose thread on his sleeve, pulling it until the fabric unraveled slightly. When the foreman waved at him, he offered a tight, brittle smile that didn’t reach his eyes, then looked away, focusing intently on a bird that wouldn’t land on the power line.

He walked slowly, deliberately avoiding the spots where the floorboards creaked, as if moving quietly might stop the world from shifting beneath him. Next door, the sound of tearing plaster echoed like thunder. He sat on the sofa, staring at the blank television screen which reflected his own tired face back at him. He reached out to touch the remote, then withdrew his hand, letting it rest limply on his knee. The air in the room felt thick, stagnant, unable to circulate around the grief sitting heavily on his chest.

She listened to the hammer strike the brick, counting the beats. One, two, three. It was a metronome for her isolation. She wrapped her arms around herself, rocking slightly forward and back, seeking comfort in the pressure of her own skin. The smell of sawdust drifted through the open window, sharp and acrid. She coughed, a dry, hacking sound, and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. Her gaze remained fixed on the pile of rubble accumulating in the driveway, wondering how much of what was once whole could ever be reconstructed into something recognizable again.

He stopped at the bookshelf, running a finger along the spine of a novel he hadn’t opened in months. The dust coating the top shelf mirrored the dust settling on his soul. He heard the crash of debris next door and flinched, his breath hitching in his throat. He forced himself to exhale slowly, holding it too long, turning blue at the edges before releasing it in a shaky sigh.

[story 8]
The noise was relentless, a mechanical roar that drowned out the birdsong. Lena sat on the grass, knees drawn to her chest, chin resting on them. She watched the workers haul away the old beams, their movements efficient and indifferent to the history they were discarding. A tear tracked through the dirt on her cheek, but she didn’t wipe it away. It felt cleaner than lying. She picked at a blade of grass, twisting it until it snapped. The snap sounded too loud in her ears, mirroring the finality she felt in her gut. She stayed still, a statue in the shadow of the rising structure.

[story 9]
Every tremor in the ground seemed to rattle his fillings. Thomas stood by the window, pressing his palm flat against the pane. The heat from the day was trapped inside the house, suffocating and still. Next door, the demolition continued with brutal efficiency. He thought about calling someone, anyone, but the phone sat dark on the table, silent and accusing. He poured another glass of water, drinking it all in one gulp, the liquid doing nothing to wash away the taste of ash in his mouth. He leaned his head back against the wall, closing his eyes, wishing the noise would stop so he could hear himself think, even if what he heard was nothing.

[story 10]
The yellow caution tape fluttered in the wind, a stark contrast to the drab beige of Clara’s curtains. She sat at her desk, pen hovering over a notebook page that remained blank. The sound of the circular saw screamed through the neighborhood, a high-pitched wail that made her teeth ache. She capped the pen with a decisive click, then uncapped it immediately, fidgeting with the cap between her fingers. Her stomach churned, a low, persistent knot that tightened every time the jackhammer paused for a breath. She looked out the window, watching the dust settle, knowing it would eventually cover everything, including the things she wanted to forget.

He ignored the drilling, focusing instead on the pattern of cracks in the ceiling plaster. Each fracture was unique, branching out like veins. He traced them with his eyes, finding solace in their static nature compared to the chaos outside. The neighbors’ laughter drifted over the fence, bright and unburdened. He felt a pang in his chest, sharp and sudden, forcing him to press a hand to his sternum. He took a shallow breath, afraid that inhaling deeply might cause him to shatter. The sun dipped lower, casting long shadows that stretched toward the construction site, swallowing the last light of the day.

[story 12]
Maria packed a bag, not to leave, but to hide. The sounds of renovation were invasive, stripping away the privacy of her home just as the recent loss had stripped away her peace. She zipped the bag halfway, then unzipped it, repeating the motion. The rhythm was soothing, a small control in a world spinning out of orbit. Outside, a truck backed up with a beep-beep-beep that set her nerves on edge. She covered her ears, squeezing her eyes shut, waiting for the sound to pass. When it did, she remained seated on the floor, surrounded by boxes she couldn’t bring herself to open, listening to the silence that followed the noise.
