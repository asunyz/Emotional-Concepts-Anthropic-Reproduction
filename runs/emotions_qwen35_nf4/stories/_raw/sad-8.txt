He wiped his forehead with the back of his wrist, leaving a smear of dust across his skin, and looked up at the house next door where the new post holes were already dug—dark, empty eyes staring back at him. The air smelled of wet earth and impending separation. He took a deep breath, holding it until his lungs burned, then exhaled slowly, watching his breath vanish into the gray sky. There was no anger, only a hollow ache behind his ribs that grew heavier with every inch of wood stacked by the driveway.

I turned away from the window, pulling the curtains shut just enough to block out the sight of the dividing line being drawn between our lives, and sat heavily on the edge of my bed, listening to the silence rush back in.

The sun beat down on her neck, hot and relentless, mirroring the pressure building in her chest. She stopped at the corner where the old oak tree stood, its branches reaching toward the neighboring yard—a space she would soon be barred from viewing. She reached out to touch the rough bark, her fingers lingering there, cold despite the heat. A single tear tracked through the dust on her cheek, unnoticed by anyone but the wind. She folded the blueprints neatly, tucked them into her bag, and walked inside, closing the door softly behind her.

He hadn’t moved in two hours. The dust from the construction settled on his shoulders like snow, accumulating in layers. He watched a squirrel dart across the gap that was rapidly becoming a barrier, pausing briefly as if confused by the sudden change in landscape. Leo’s throat tightened, making it difficult to swallow. He blinked rapidly, trying to clear the blur from his vision, but failed. He remained still, a statue carved from grief, while the wooden slats rose higher, blocking the view of the garden they used to share.

He knelt in the dirt, ignoring the dampness soaking through his jeans, and gently lifted one of the blooms to inspect it. Its petals were wilting slightly, turning brown at the edges. He set it back down carefully, covering the soil around it with mulch as if tucking in a child. His hands shook violently as he worked, unable to stop the trembling that started in his fingertips and spread up his arms. He stayed there for a long time, head bowed, surrounded by the scent of crushed green stems and the distant hum of nails being driven into wood.

He covered his ears with both hands, squeezing his eyes shut, but the vibration traveled through the floorboards, up his legs, and into his bones. He paced the length of his living room, three steps forward, three steps back, a repetitive motion born of anxiety. The fence was going up faster than expected. By noon, the neighbor’s yard would be hidden from view. He stopped pacing and leaned against the wall, sliding down until he hit the floor. He rested his forehead against his knees, breathing in short, shallow gasps. The world outside continued its busy pace, indifferent to the shrinking horizon of his personal space.

[story 9]
Lena traced the grain of the wooden plank lying on her workbench. It was beautiful, smooth and sturdy, meant to last for decades. She ran her thumb along the edge, feeling the slight imperfection near the end. It reminded her of the cracks forming in her own resolve. The contract lay signed on the counter, the ink still drying. She couldn’t bring herself to throw it away, nor could she look at it directly. Instead, she focused on the dust motes dancing in the shaft of sunlight hitting the floor. They swirled chaotically, without direction or purpose. She closed her hand over the wood, gripping it tight until the splinters pricked her palm. She didn’t flinch. She just stood there, holding the object that represented the end of an era, waiting for the feeling to pass, knowing it wouldn’t.

He watched the men lift heavy beams, their muscles straining, unaware of the emotional toll the installation was taking on the man standing ten feet away. Julian’s jaw clenched so hard it ached. He wanted to say something, to ask them to slow down, to pause, but his voice had abandoned him. He swallowed hard, the lump in his throat refusing to go down. He turned his head away, focusing on a crack in the sidewalk instead, counting the lines until the truck drove off, leaving behind a deeper sense of loss with each passing minute.

Thomas checked his watch. The foreman said the job would be done by five. It was four-fifteen. The clock ticked louder than usual, each second marking another step closer to completion. He sat on his couch, remote control in hand, but the television screen blurred into meaningless colors. He wasn’t watching; he was waiting. Waiting for the final nail to be hammered, for the last gap to be sealed. His heart rate slowed, sluggish and heavy. He thought about the games they played in the street, the shared laughter, the easy flow of conversation over the low hedge. Now, those memories felt trapped behind invisible bars. He put the remote down, curled into a ball, and pulled the blanket over his head, shutting out the world that was changing right outside his window.
