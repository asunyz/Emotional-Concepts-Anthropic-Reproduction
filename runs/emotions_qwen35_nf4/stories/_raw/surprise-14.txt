The chair was pushed back slightly, as if its occupant had just stepped away for coffee, yet the dust motes dancing in the afternoon sun suggested otherwise. His fingers trembled as they brushed against the cold leather of the seat, tracing the faint outline of a name stitched into the fabric. He looked around the workshop, expecting to see Thorne’s glasses perched on a workbench or his half-finished sketch lying open. Silence stretched heavy between the shelves of tools, broken only by the ticking of the wall clock. Elias felt a hollow ache expand in his chest, a sudden void where the familiar rhythm of instruction used to be. He reached for the phone on the wall, dialing the number he knew by heart, but the line rang out into nothingness before connecting.

Detective Miller kicked the door open, expecting to find his partner arguing over a case file. Instead, he found a single key resting on the center of the desk, next to a neatly folded uniform shirt. No badge. No gun belt. Just the shirt, pressed and waiting. Miller’s jaw tightened, the muscles bunching under his skin as he picked up the shirt. It was too clean, too organized. He flipped through the drawers, finding them emptied of personal effects—photos, mugs, family pictures—all gone. The absence was loud, ringing in his ears louder than any siren. He dropped the shirt onto the floor, turning sharply to face the window. Outside, the city bustled on, indifferent to the fact that one of its most reliable anchors had simply vanished. He gripped the windowsill until his knuckles turned white, staring at the reflection of his own confused face, wondering what could possibly drive a man to abandon his post without a word.

Captain Reynolds sat at his desk, staring at the resignation letter propped up against the lamp. It was unsigned, just a date and a signature line that remained blank. He tapped his pen against the wood, the rhythmic tapping growing faster, more erratic. He had trained this officer for two years, instilling discipline, strategy, honor. To leave without notice, without a debriefing—it was insubordination, yes, but also a profound betrayal of trust. He stood up abruptly, knocking the pen to the floor. He didn’t bend to pick it up. Instead, he walked to the map on the wall, tracing the routes they had planned together. The lines seemed meaningless now, abstract scribbles without a leader to guide them. A flush of heat rose to his neck, frustration boiling over. He slammed his fist against the map, the paper tearing slightly. The noise startled him, but he didn’t care. The silence of the departed officer screamed louder than any explosion.

He pushed through the crowd, asking guests if they had seen her. Shakes of heads. Furrowed brows. Confusion rippled through him, spreading from his chest to his fingertips. He went backstage, pushing past the crew, into the dressing room. Empty. Her gown lay draped over a chair, her shoes beside it. No note. No explanation. He touched the silk of the dress, the fabric cool and slippery under his fingers. A wave of nausea hit him, forcing him to lean against the wall for support. The spotlight outside burned bright, waiting for a performer who wouldn’t come. The anticipation curdled into anxiety, twisting his stomach into knots as he realized the show was going on without her.
