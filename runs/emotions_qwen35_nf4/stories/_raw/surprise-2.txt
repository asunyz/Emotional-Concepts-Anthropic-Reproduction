There, sketched in crayon by his five-year-old niece, was Barnaby. Not just any bear, but *the* bear with the crooked ear and the missing button eye—the one Elias had spoken to for ten years before he decided growing up meant silence. He rubbed his temples, trying to force the image out of his mind, but the drawing seemed to pulse under his gaze. "Who drew this?" he asked, his voice cracking slightly. The little girl looked up, blinking innocently. "Mr. Fluff," she said. Elias felt the floor tilt beneath him, the air suddenly too thin to breathe.

I stared at the paper taped to the refrigerator, my hand frozen halfway to grabbing the milk carton. It wasn’t possible. The lines were jagged, childish, yet unmistakably the silhouette of Silas, the shadow-figure that used to sit on the edge of my bed when I was seven. My heart hammered against my ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage. I leaned closer, squinting until the ink blurred. Was this a coincidence? A trick of the light? I reached out, trembling fingers hovering inches from the page, afraid to touch it as if it might burn me. "Mommy says it’s just a monster," my nephew whispered behind me. I didn’t answer. I couldn’t. My mouth had gone completely dry, tasting only copper and fear.

The chalk dust hung in the air, glittering in the afternoon sun as Clara wiped her hands on her apron. She turned around, expecting to see her daughter playing with blocks, but instead found her standing before the driveway pavement, erasing a figure with a frantic sweep of her foot. Clara stepped forward, peering over the child’s shoulder. The erased smudge revealed a faint outline of a creature with six legs and glowing eyes—a detail Clara knew intimately because she had drawn it herself in a diary hidden for decades. Her stomach dropped. She knelt down, her knees popping loudly in the quiet street. "Where did you learn to draw that?" she asked, keeping her tone light, though her pulse roared in her ears. The girl shrugged, kicking a pebble. "He showed me." Clara stood up slowly, her legs feeling like water, scanning the empty yard for anyone else.

Marcus gripped the steering wheel until his knuckles turned white. The car idled outside the elementary school, but he hadn’t moved in twenty minutes. On the passenger seat lay a folder of art projects collected from the day. He flipped past the standard landscapes and self-portraits until he stopped cold. There, in meticulous pencil strokes, was the face of Old Man Jenkins’ dog, Buster, but with human hands. Marcus remembered Buster vividly; he had been the only thing that comforted him during the hospital stays. He blinked hard, rubbing his eyes, assuming fatigue was playing tricks on him. But the drawing remained, stark and undeniable. He exhaled sharply, a long, shuddering breath that fogged the windshield. Why would a seven-year-old know about a dog that died thirty years ago? He started the engine, the noise loud in the stillness, and drove away without picking up his niece.

David laughed nervously, a short, brittle sound that echoed in the empty kitchen. He pointed at the tablet screen where his niece had uploaded her digital artwork. "That looks exactly like Captain Zog," he said, leaning in closer. "But... how do you know what he looks like? I made him up in my head. No one ever saw him except me." He waited for an explanation, a joke, anything rational. The girl tilted her head, frowning. "He talks to me when I sleep," she replied matter-of-factly. David pulled back, his chair scraping harshly against the tile floor. He pushed himself up, pacing toward the window, needing space, needing distance from the impossible statement. His hands shook as he fumbled with the blinds, pulling them shut to block out the world. He pressed his forehead against the cool glass, closing his eyes tight, wishing he could wake up from whatever dream this was.

I crept down quietly, the wood creaking under my weight. When I got close enough to see the subject, my blood ran cold. It was the Gargoyle from the library roof, the one I used to visit to escape bullies. I had never mentioned it to anyone. I had never taken a photo. Yet here it was, rendered in wax crayon with terrifying accuracy. I reached out to pull the paper away, but my hand stopped inches from the surface. What if looking at it makes it real? What if it decides to come down from the page? I withdrew my hand slowly, backing away until my spine hit the wall. I slid down to the floor, hugging my knees, unable to look away from the creature’s stone-cold stare.

My grandmother adjusted her glasses, squinting at the piece of paper held between her wrinkled fingers. "This is Mrs. Higgins," she whispered, her voice trembling. "She lived next door when we were girls. She passed away forty years ago." The drawing was crude, done by my young cousin, but the resemblance was uncanny. Grandma’s eyes widened, filling with tears that spilled over onto her cheeks. She began to shake, a fine vibration that traveled through her entire frame. She dropped the drawing, catching it reflexively before it hit the ground. "How does she know?" she breathed, looking around the room as if searching for an accomplice. The house felt suddenly smaller, the shadows longer. Grandmother sat down heavily in her armchair, clutching the drawing to her chest like a lifeline. She wouldn’t let go. None of us dared to take it from her.

The gallery opening was loud, filled with clinking glasses and polite chatter. I was sipping champagne when a familiar shape caught my eye on a display stand. It was a print of a charcoal sketch. The subject was The Watcher, a nameless entity I had dreamed about every night for a week after my father left. I set the glass down on a passing tray, the ice clinking loudly. I approached the artwork, ignoring the people brushing past me. Up close, the detail was exquisite. The hollow eyes, the twisted posture—it was my nightmare given form. My skin prickled with goosebumps, though the room was warm. I looked around wildly, searching for the artist. Did someone steal my journal? Was this a public exhibition of my private madness? I backed away, bumping into a waiter. I muttered an apology that came out as a gasp, then fled the room, bursting out into the night air, needing to run until my lungs burned.
