Arthur’s knuckles were white as he gripped the edge of the kitchen counter, his breath coming in short, sharp hitches that fogged the air between him and the refrigerator door. He stared at the crayon sketch taped to the fridge, the one his seven-year-old niece had left there after school. The figure was unmistakable: tall, lanky, with hair that defied gravity and eyes drawn in frantic, jagged scribbles. It was Barnaby. The name echoed in Arthur’s skull, loud and intrusive, dragging up decades of suppressed memories he had buried under layers of adult responsibility. His jaw clenched so hard a muscle twitched visibly near his ear. He reached out, not to touch the paper, but to slam his palm against the cabinet above it, the wood vibrating with the force of his impact. "No," he muttered, the word grinding out like gravel. "Absolutely not." He turned away from the drawing, pacing the length of the room until his boots scuffed the floorboards, leaving faint gray marks behind. Every step felt heavy, weighed down by the sudden, suffocating realization that the past wasn’t dead; it was just waiting for an audience.
