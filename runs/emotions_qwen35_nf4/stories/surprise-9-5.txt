I couldn’t breathe. The air in the hallway felt thin, recycled, stale. I gripped the strap of my laptop bag until my tendons popped. Mom was standing in the doorway of the guest room, which I had assumed would be mine. But the bed was made with military precision, the pillows plumped symmetrically. On the nightstand lay a welcome basket: apples, cheese, and a brochure for local therapy centers. My jaw went slack. I blinked rapidly, trying to clear the fog from my mind. "Therapy?" I whispered, the word tasting bitter. Dad appeared behind her, holding a tool belt. "We thought you might need some support adjusting," he said, his tone practical, devoid of malice. That was the worst part. They weren’t angry. They were helpful. And that helplessness washed over me, chilling my bones. I looked at the brochure, then at their expectant faces. My mouth opened, but no sound came out. I nodded slowly, a jerky, mechanical motion, and stepped into the room, closing the door softly behind me, leaning against it as my legs gave way.
